▸ 01 · Prologue

What This Doc Is, And Who It's For

This document is a system explainer. It teaches what every agent and every tool is and does, using designed worked-example illustrations computed from documented multipliers. For the actual numbers a reviewer can reproduce, see §18 Live runs — six end-to-end captured runs (both demo profiles × new-quote / explain / counterfactual) with LangSmith trace URLs and the raw output.json + stderr.log per scenario. It is written for the reviewer, the auditor, the customer's attorney, or the new hire who would like the architectural picture before reading source code.

Two demo profiles run through the whole document as recurring examples:

  • Profile A — California, $900,000 dwelling, swimming pool, one prior claim, credit score 700. The marquee Prop 103 case. The credit score gets dropped before pricing ever sees it.
  • Profile B — Florida, $450,000 dwelling, no pool, no prior claims, credit_score: null. The marquee §626.9741 case. The null credit gets a neutral 1.0× multiplier instead of a worst-case penalty.

The unifying thread is data honesty. The pipeline produces premiums that look like real insurance quotes, but underneath, only a small minority of values come from a live data source at quote time. The rest are synthetic-but-calibrated against published industry data — and the project documents this explicitly in data/REAL_DATA_PROVENANCE.md. The system's strength is not that every number is real; it is that every number carries an evidence_id pointing back to a row in a documented table, and every table is honestly labeled as real, calibrated, or synthetic. A regulator asking "where did this $5,735 come from?" gets a defensible answer.

Reading order. If you have ten minutes, read Section 02 (the mall), Section 03 (the statutes), and Section 04 (credit suppression). If you have thirty, add Section 05 (the cast). If you have an hour, read the whole thing — Section 07 (the pricing tools, source-verified) is the payoff for anyone who wants to understand exactly which numbers came from where.

▸ 03 · The Insurance Mall

Four Market Tiers, Dissected

Before any premium calculation, the pipeline answers one gating question: which market should this customer buy from? In US homeowners insurance there are four candidates, each with different rules, regulators, costs, and use cases. Picking the wrong one breaks everything downstream — wrong premium, wrong coverage, possibly an outright decline. EligibilityTriage is the agent that picks; this section explains what the four candidates are.

1 · ADMITTED — the main department store

Who runs it. Private licensed carriers — State Farm, Allstate, Mercury, Travelers, Liberty Mutual, USAA, Farmers, AAA, Nationwide. The state Department of Insurance has reviewed their financials, approved their rates, and licensed them to sell.

Who it's for. "Normal" customers — homes that fit the carrier's underwriting appetite. Moderate risk, average-condition home, average claims history.

Cost. Cheapest of the four tiers. Competition is fierce and the state forces every dollar of every rate to be justified with actuarial data.

Regulated. Heavily. Rates filed and often pre-approved before use. Twelve to twenty-four months from filing to effective date is normal. Policy forms are state-approved.

What customers gain. Three things no other tier offers: guaranty fund protection (if the carrier goes bankrupt, the state's fund pays your claim up to a cap), state-approved policy forms (standardized language vetted by DOI lawyers), and rate-justification discipline (the carrier had to prove the rate isn't excessive or unfairly discriminatory).

When admitted will not work. The carrier's underwriting rules say no. In CA that is usually a home in a Fire Hazard Severity Zone of "High" or "Very High" — most admitted carriers stopped writing those zones after the 2017-2018 wildfire losses. In FL it is usually a coastal county where carriers have hit capacity, or a roof old enough that wind-mitigation can't bring the risk back to admitted appetite.

2 · FAIR_DIC — the California safety-net with a private add-on

Who runs it. Two things bundled together. (1) The California FAIR Plan, a non-profit insurer-of-last-resort pool created by California in 1968, funded by every admitted carrier in the state in proportion to their market share. (2) A private DIC ("Difference-in-Conditions") policy that wraps around the FAIR Plan to fill its coverage gaps.

Who it's for. CA homes that admitted carriers refuse — usually because the property sits in a "High" or "Very High" Fire Hazard Severity Zone under CAL FIRE's mapping. After the 2017-2018 wildfires this means a great many California homes: entire towns in Sonoma, Napa, Lake County, parts of LA County.

Cost. Higher than admitted. FAIR Plan basic alone is roughly 1.4-1.8× admitted-equivalent, and a DIC wrap adds another 30-50% on top. Total is typically 1.6-2.0× admitted.

Regulated. The FAIR Plan itself is heavily regulated — it's a creature of state law. The DIC wrap is on the surplus-lines side and is more lightly regulated.

The catch. The FAIR Plan policy is a DP-1 dwelling-fire policy — fire, lightning, and internal explosion only. No theft. No water damage from burst pipes. No personal liability. Limited loss-of-use. Limited personal property. The DIC wrap (sold by surplus-lines carriers like Lloyd's syndicates) is what fills these gaps so the combination approximates an admitted HO-3.

History. The FAIR Plan was created in 1968 in the aftermath of the Watts riots, when private carriers refused to insure homes in certain neighborhoods. California's answer was a mandatory risk-sharing pool. The 2024 Sustainable Insurance Strategy was created in part because so many CA homeowners were being pushed into FAIR_DIC after admitted carriers fled wildfire zones.

3 · CITIZENS — the Florida state-owned store

Who runs it. Citizens Property Insurance Corporation — Florida's state-run insurance company. Created by the FL Legislature in 2002 by merging two earlier last-resort programs. Owned by the state of Florida. The board is appointed by FL government officials and the company is heavily regulated by the FL Office of Insurance Regulation.

Who it's for. FL homes that admitted private carriers won't write — usually because the home is in a high-hurricane-exposure county (Miami-Dade, Monroe, Broward, Palm Beach, parts of the Panhandle), or because admitted carriers in that ZIP code have hit their capacity limits.

Cost. Capped by statute (Fla. Stat. §627.351 — the "glide path" rule limits Citizens to the highest of the top 20 private carriers in a given territory), but the cap is being raised yearly because Citizens has been under-charging relative to the real hurricane risk. Recent law allows ~14% per-policy annual rate increases.

Regulated. State-owned and OIR-regulated. The 2026 rate filing is the public exception — most carrier rate filings are sensitive, Citizens' is public.

What makes Citizens different from CA's FAIR Plan. Three things. (1) It is a real insurance company, not just a risk-sharing pool — its own adjusters, its own staff, its own systems. (2) It sells real HO-3 policies, not bare DP-1. Liability, theft, water damage, loss-of-use are all included. (3) Its rate is capped — but that is a problem, not a feature. Every major hurricane season eats into Citizens' reserves and the legislature has been steadily allowing larger annual rate increases to "depopulate" Citizens (move customers back to private carriers). As of late 2025 Citizens holds approximately 1.2 million policies.

Statutory features baked into Citizens policies. Hurricane deductible options under §627.701 (must offer $500 / 2% / 5% / 10% of dwelling value), mandatory CGCC under §627.706 (Catastrophic Ground Cover Collapse), optional sinkhole coverage under §627.706(2) (must be offered separately, signed rejection notice required).

4 · SURPLUS_LINES — the specialty store of last resort

Who runs it. Non-admitted carriers — companies that have not been licensed by the state DOI but are still allowed to write policies through specially-licensed surplus-lines brokers. The famous examples are Lloyd's of London syndicates, Lexington Insurance (AIG subsidiary), Scottsdale Insurance (Nationwide subsidiary), Markel International.

Who it's for. Anyone the other three tiers reject. Multi-million-dollar coastal estates that exceed admitted carrier limits. Homes with serious risk markers (multiple prior claims, historic property with non-standard wiring, exotic features). Homes in jurisdictions with no admitted appetite at all.

Cost. Most expensive. No rate caps, no DOI rate review. The premium is whatever the underwriter writes on the binder. Typically 1.6-2.4× admitted-equivalent, sometimes much higher for unusual risks.

Regulated. Lightly. Two things customers should know: there is no state guaranty fund coverage (if the surplus-lines carrier goes bankrupt, the customer eats the loss), and the customer pays a state-specific surplus-lines tax (typically 3-5%) on top of premium.

Why it exists. If surplus lines didn't exist, customers with unusual risks would be uninsurable. The state's bargain is "we'll let unregulated carriers serve you, but you give up consumer protections." Access is through a surplus-lines broker — the customer cannot buy direct.

Warnings the system must surface. The output's warnings[] array must include the no-guaranty-fund disclosure, the surplus-lines tax disclosure, and the policy-forms-not-state-approved disclosure when this route is chosen.

Picking the wrong tier breaks everything downstream:

  • Pricing the wrong policy form (FAIR Plan DP-1 vs admitted HO-3) → customer rejects the quote.
  • Recommending coverages the policy form doesn't carry → regulatory complaint, bad-faith claim.
  • Applying admitted-market multipliers to a surplus-lines quote → underprices by 60-140%.
  • Telling the customer they have HO-3 open-peril when the policy is actually FAIR Plan basic → potential E&O lawsuit when the first claim is denied.

This is why EligibilityTriage (covered in Section 05) treats the route decision as a gate, persists all four candidate scores in the DecisionTrace for audit, and gives statutorily-triggered rules a path to override the deterministic pick.

Why EligibilityTriage runs here — at position 3

EligibilityTriage sits at position 3 in the pipeline — right after StatutoryAgent (the law) and before Risk, Coverage, and Pricing. This placement is load-bearing: every later position would force backtracking, wasted LLM calls, or an incoherent quote.

What breaks if you move Eligibility later

If Eligibility ran AFTER…What breaks downstream
RiskAgentRisk wastes effort scoring perils the market doesn't cover. FAIR Plan's DP-1 form has no theft, no water, no liability — why probe those hazards? A market-blind RiskAgent fires every probe and the irrelevant ones land in the output as noise.

What it needs (and what it doesn't)

EligibilityTriage's inputs — state, location, home_value, plus triggered_rules and market_route_hints from StatutoryAgent — are all available the moment StatutoryAgent finishes. Hazard scores from RiskAgent don't change market admissibility: a Very-High FHSZ home in California is FAIR-only whether wildfire is rated HIGH or MEDIUM. Eligibility is a structural property of the home + jurisdiction, not a function of risk severity.

The counterfactual angle (DEC-0010)

When a customer asks "what if I removed the pool?", the system forks state and re-runs only Risk → Coverage → Pricing. StatutoryAgent and EligibilityTriage do not re-run. The law doesn't change when you remove a pool; the market route doesn't change either — admittedness is a property of the home + jurisdiction, not the pool. Putting Eligibility upstream of the fork point seals it: a customer cannot toggle their way into a cheaper market by changing a feature. That's the dashed amber zone in the diagram above.

Why all 4 ToT branches stay in DecisionTrace

EligibilityTriage scores all four market candidates, prunes losers, picks a winner — but all four scores live in DecisionTrace afterward. That payload powers three downstream consumers without re-running the agent: (i) audit answers ("why didn't you put me in admitted?" → branch scores + prune reasons), (ii) Council re-examination when the Validator flags a problem, (iii) counterfactual reuse if a fork mutates a field that would have changed the route.

▸ 04 · The Two Statutes

The Two Statutes That Shape Everything

Two state laws govern what insurers may and may not consider when pricing a homeowners policy in this system's two supported jurisdictions. They are cited everywhere in the codebase because they are the highest-priority compliance constraints — violating either one is a regulatory failure, not just a bad quote. The two statutes apply to the same field (credit score) and reach the same operational outcome (credit doing zero pricing work for both demo profiles), but by completely different statutory paths.

California Proposition 103 — Cal. Ins. Code §§1861.01–1861.16 (1988)

30-second backstory. In 1988, California voters were furious about runaway auto and homeowners insurance rates and passed Proposition 103 by a 51% ballot vote. It became one of the strictest rate-regulation regimes in the United States and still governs every homeowners policy sold in California, ~38 years later.

§1861.05 — Prior-approval rates. "No rate shall be approved or remain in effect which is excessive, inadequate, or unfairly discriminatory." In practice: insurers cannot just raise rates. They must file proposed rates with the California Department of Insurance, supply actuarial data justifying them, and wait for approval. Rate changes can take 12-24 months from filing to effective date. Consumers can challenge filings; the Department can hold hearings.

§1861.02 + Cal. Code Regs. tit. 10 §2632.5 — Prohibited rating factors. Several factors cannot be used as a primary basis for rates:

  • Credit-based insurance scores are prohibited (§2632.5). The Coverage and Pricing agents must drop credit_score from their context entirely.
  • Age, gender, marital status, ethnicity, religion, national origin (§1861.02).
  • ZIP code alone (§1861.02(a), extended to homeowners by the 2024 Sustainable Insurance Strategy).

§10081 — Earthquake offer requirement. "Every insurer offering residential property insurance shall offer earthquake coverage at every new business and renewal." Most insurers satisfy this through the California Earthquake Authority (CEA) Companion Policy program — the insurer markets the CEA policy without underwriting it themselves.

2024 Sustainable Insurance Strategy. Commissioner Lara's December 2023 – January 2024 framework allowing forward-looking catastrophe models and net-of-reinsurance ratemaking in exchange for required writings in distressed wildfire markets. Marks the first significant CAT-modeling permission in CA personal lines.

What our system does about it. The StatutoryAgent retrieves chunk CDI-PROP103-01 from the ca_doi corpus on every CA quote, fires CA-PROP103-CREDIT with action drop_field('credit_score'), and writes field_treatments['credit_score'] = 'dropped_by_CA-PROP103-CREDIT'. Every downstream agent sees a sanitized profile with credit_score=None. The Compliance Council holds VETO power as a second backstop.

Florida Statutes §626.9741 — Use of Credit Reports and Credit Scores by Insurers

30-second backstory. Florida (unlike California) allows insurers to use credit-based insurance scores in personal-lines pricing. The guardrail is that customers without credit information cannot be punished for it. The statute is enforced by the Florida Department of Financial Services.

§626.9741(7) — Neutral treatment when credit is unavailable. "If a credit report is unavailable, the insurer shall apply a neutral credit factor." In practice that is a 1.0× multiplier on the credit-banded portion of the rate calculation. The insurer cannot default to "worst-case" credit assumptions, cannot decline coverage for lack of credit, cannot substitute a different proxy.

§626.9741(8) — Refresh rule. Insurers must order credit information at new business and at every other renewal (~24 months). If the bureau returns "no hit" or "thin file," §626.9741(7) kicks in (neutral 1.0×). If the bureau returns a real score, the insurer uses it normally.

§626.9741(2) — Prohibited inputs into the credit score itself. The credit score model itself cannot use as a primary factor: income, gender, address, ZIP code, ethnic group, religion, marital status, nationality. This is a fairness rule on the credit-scoring vendors (FICO, LexisNexis Risk Solutions, etc.), not on the insurer directly.

Adverse-action notice requirement. Insurers using credit information must provide adverse-action notice to any consumer whose rate is increased due to credit, aligning with FCRA notice requirements but enforced separately by FL DFS. Mortgage applicants may request a re-rating when credit information improves materially.

What our system does about it. The StatutoryAgent retrieves chunk FLDFS-CREDIT-01 from the fl_dfs corpus on every FL quote where credit is null, fires FL-CREDIT-NEUTRAL with action flag_field_treatment('credit_score', treatment='neutral_1.0x'), and the PricingAgent's pricing_multiplier_lookup(credit_score, neutral_1.0x) returns exactly 1.00 from MULT-CREDIT-NEUTRAL-FL626. The customer with no credit prices identically to one with average credit.

▸ 05 · Credit Suppression

Same Field, Opposite Laws, One Quiet Pipeline

Grep the source tree for credit_score and you will find it declared in the input schema, talked about in the StatutoryAgent prompt, explained in the corpus markdown, and tested in the regression suite. You will not find any pricing-relevant agent doing if profile.credit_score < 650: multiplier = 1.25. That code would be a bug. It would violate Prop 103 in California and §626.9741(7) in Florida. The architecture intentionally makes credit-score pricing invisible from the outside in — by the time any pricing-relevant code runs, the field is either gone or labeled neutral.

The two-step contract

Step 1 — StatutoryAgent emits an action. For CA profiles it emits a drop_field action targeting credit_score. For FL profiles with credit_score=null it emits a flag_field_treatment action with treatment 'neutral_1.0x'. The action is replayed deterministically in _emission_to_output() (agents/statutory_agent.py:329-333):

if atype == "drop_field" and a.field:
    sanitized[a.field] = None                                       # CA: credit_score = None
    out.field_treatments[a.field] = f"dropped_by_{r.rule_id}"      # → 'dropped_by_CA-PROP103-CREDIT'

elif atype == "flag_field_treatment" and a.field and a.treatment:
    out.field_treatments[a.field] = a.treatment                     # FL: 'neutral_1.0x'

Step 2 — PricingAgent keys on the treatment label, never the raw value. The planner reads the field_treatments dict and emits the right multiplier-lookup task (agents/pricing_agent.py:99-104):

credit_treatment = field_treatments.get("credit_score") or "neutral_1.0x"
credit_key = "neutral_1.0x" if credit_treatment.startswith("neutral") \
             else "dropped"  if credit_treatment.startswith("dropped") \
             else "neutral_1.0x"
tasks.append(
    PricingTask(step_id=f"#E{next_id}", tool="pricing_multiplier_lookup",
                inputs={"dimension": "credit_score", "key": credit_key})
)

The defensive default at the end (else "neutral_1.0x") is the belt-and-suspenders. Even if field_treatments is missing or carries an unexpected value, the fallback is the safe (compliance-respecting) one. The multiplier lookup returns 1.00 for both dropped and neutral_1.0x keys — both anchored explicitly to their statutes in pricing_multipliers.json:

Compliance lives in the data file. Those two 1.00 entries are the entire compliance contract. If a future engineer accidentally changes them to anything else, three independent backstops fire: (1) the Phase-4 self-check in StatutoryAgent flags the discrepancy, (2) the Compliance Officer Council persona VETOes the quote, (3) tests/verify_replacements.py fails in CI.

🔵 5.1 · IntentClassifier — the front door

Reads the user's message (or its absence) and decides which lane the graph runs.

What it does

Think of it as the receptionist at a clinic. Before anyone sees a doctor, the receptionist asks "are you here for a new visit, a follow-up question, or something else?" and routes you to the right room. The IntentClassifier asks the same question of every incoming request and routes it to one of four lanes: NEW_QUOTE (fresh customer), EXPLANATION ("why is this quote expensive?"), COUNTERFACTUAL ("what if I removed the pool?"), or OUT_OF_SCOPE (the question is not about insurance at all).

The pattern is Self-Ask — the LLM is forced to answer four ordered binary sub-questions in writing before committing to a label. The decomposition is in the prompt and the answers are captured in the trace so a reviewer can see why a particular label was chosen.

⚡ 5.6 · PricingAgent — the layer cake builder

Calculates the premium by chaining a base premium with a series of risk multipliers, each looked up from real pricing tables, each carrying a citation. Produces a low/high premium range and a plain-English factor chain.

What it does

Builds the premium like a layer cake. The base layer is the state-and-year average premium for a $250K reference home (~$1,976 for CA 2026, ~$5,735 for FL 2026). Layered on top: home-value scaling (a $900K home pays 2.17× more than a $250K home), wildfire multiplier, seismic multiplier, claims surcharge, pool surcharge, credit multiplier (always 1.00 for our two demo profiles, per the suppression contract from Section 04). The product of all the layers is the point estimate; apply ±25% to get the range.

The pattern is ReWOO (Reasoning WithOut Observation): a Planner emits the whole task DAG up front; Workers run all tool lookups in parallel with no LLM calls between them; a Solver combines results deterministically and lets one LLM call polish the explanation prose. Numbers are protected — the LLM can never change the math, only the wording.

The StatutoryAgent's entire job depends on retrieving the right statute prose at the right moment. ▸ 09 · Orchestration Flow

How LangGraph Sequences Agents And Handles Dependencies

The orchestrator is LangGraph. The pipeline is built as a StateGraph over a TypedDict GraphState. Each agent is a Python function that reads slices of state and returns a dict; LangGraph merges the dict into the next state using per-field reducers. Conditional edges fork the graph based on the IntentClassifier's chosen lane. A MemorySaver SQLite checkpoint persists state by thread_id, which is what lets Follow-up and Counterfactual answer follow-up questions without re-running the upstream pipeline.

The four lanes

IntentSequenceApproximate cost / latency
NEW_QUOTEIntent → Statutory → Eligibility → Risk → Coverage → Pricing (Planner → Workers×9 in parallel → Solver) → Validator → (Council if escalated) → Confidence → output_assembler~$0.025 / ~22s
EXPLANATIONIntent → FollowupExplain → output_assembler~$0.005 / ~3s (5× cheaper)
COUNTERFACTUALIntent → Counterfactual (forks state, re-runs Risk → Coverage → Pricing) → output_assembler~$0.015 / ~12s
OUT_OF_SCOPEIntent → refusal_node → output_assembler~$0.001 / <1s

Dependency handling

  • State merge. Each agent returns a dict; LangGraph applies a reducer per field. Most fields use last-write-wins; some use specialized reducers.
  • Parallel fan-out. The PricingPlanner emits a list of tasks; the runtime dispatches each task via LangGraph's Send primitive to a separate worker invocation. Results merge back via operator.add on the pricing_results field.
  • DecisionTrace. Every agent appends one or more nodes to state["decision_trace"]. The reducer is append_node with NEW_QUOTE reset semantics: when the IntentClassifier returns NEW_QUOTE on a thread that already has a trace, the prior trace is cleared so a fresh quote starts with a clean audit.
  • Conditional edges. Routing predicates live in graph.py. The IntentClassifier's output triggers an edge to one of four downstream lanes; the Validator's escalation triggers an edge to the Council when checks fail; the Council's VETO triggers an edge to refer_to_human=True writing.
  • Per-thread persistence. A MemorySaver SQLite backend keyed by thread_id stores state after every node. On a Follow-up or Counterfactual invocation, the runtime restores the prior state before the IntentClassifier runs, so the agents downstream see the prior quote's decision_trace, pricing_factor_chain, and sanitized_profile.

Every get_llm(role) call in the system returns a BudgetedChatModel proxy (llm_registry.py:137-247) that wraps the underlying LangChain chat model with pre-flight and post-flight token checks. check_pre_flight counts input tokens via tiktoken before the call; check_post_flight reads usage_metadata.total_tokens (or response_metadata.token_usage.total_tokens) after. ▸ 10 · Non-determinism

Pinning Down What LLMs Can And Cannot Decide

Multi-agent LLM systems are inherently non-deterministic: temperature sampling, retrieval ranking, and ReAct trajectory variation all introduce drift. Audit-defensible insurance quoting cannot tolerate that drift. The system uses three concrete techniques to compress the non-determinism: temperature 0 decoding, tool-side determinism, and LLM-can-never-override-numbers guardrails. The remainder is captured in the decision trace so any residual variation is visible.

What we implemented

  • LLM sampling temperature = 0 for every agent. This makes single-call decoding deterministic within a provider's model snapshot.
  • Random seed via CLI --seed N flag. Threaded through to LangSmith and to any deterministic sampling code (e.g., the synthetic-fallback engines).
  • Tools are 100% deterministic. All 5 pricing tools, all 7 hazard tools, all 6 coverage tools, and the RAG retriever are pure Python with CSV/JSON lookups and pure arithmetic. None calls an LLM internally.
  • Numbers are protected from LLM override. The PricingSolver computes point, low, high deterministically in Python before any LLM call. The LLM polish pass is explicitly instructed (and explicitly enforced at pricing_agent.py:317-318) to write prose only — numeric fields are not adopted from the LLM output.
  • Idempotency dedup by step_id. The PricingSolver dedupes pricing_results by step_id, keeping only the latest occurrence per step. This prevents follow-up turns from compounding multipliers when MemorySaver restores prior state.
  • Structured-output schemas. Every LLM call uses Pydantic with_structured_output to enforce shape at the SDK boundary. Malformed JSON is rejected before it reaches the graph; the agent falls back to its safety net.

Why

  • Audit-defensibility. The same input must produce the same chain of evidence_ids. A regulator asking "is this quote reproducible?" must get a yes.
  • Regression-testability. tests/verify_replacements.py asserts that Profile A drops credit and Profile B applies neutral 1.0×. Drift would break CI.
  • Compliance certainty. Prop 103 + §626.9741 must fire on every applicable run, every time. Non-determinism in the StatutoryAgent's ReAct loop is acceptable only if it converges to the same emitted rule set — which the Phase 4 self-check enforces.

Limitations (the residual non-determinism we did not eliminate)

  • Provider-snapshot drift. Temperature 0 is "mostly" but not "perfectly" deterministic across provider model versions. OpenAI's snapshot updates can produce different decodings even at temp=0. Mitigation: pin to a specific model snapshot in production.
  • ReAct trajectory variation. The StatutoryAgent's and RiskAgent's ReAct loops may pick different retrieval queries on subsequent runs (different reasoning order, same final state). Two different orderings can produce the same final rule set, so the outputs converge even when the intermediate trajectory varies.
  • Tool order in parallel fan-out. PricingWorkers run in parallel and the order of return is non-deterministic. The Solver's dedup-by-step_id removes this as a numerical concern, but the order of entries in the audit trace can vary.
  • LangSmith captures the trajectory. Any drift is visible: the full ReAct trajectory, every tool call, every retrieval, every LLM input/output is recorded. A reviewer can compare two runs side-by-side and inspect where they diverged.
  • Deterministic safety nets are strict. When the LLM fails and the deterministic engine fires, the output is provably deterministic — same input, same output, byte-for-byte. The non-determinism is bounded to the LLM-success path.

Event types:

  • passed — no firing; the happy path. Every guardrail emits this on a clean request.

  • fallback — mitigation exhausted and the deterministic safety net fired (e.g., retry budget exceeded, token budget breached with action_on_breach=fallback).

▸ 12 · Confidence Score

A 4-Signal Deterministic Aggregator (v1.0)

Confidence is not a raw LLM self-assessment. The number is computed deterministically from 4 equally-weighted signals, with a single hard cap on statutory non-compliance. The LLM cannot change the number. DEC-0004 fixes the deterministic aggregator. [original 8-signal + LLM rationale → §17]

The 4 signals + their weights (v1.0)

SignalWeightWhat it measuresHow it scores
validation_pass_rate0.25No statutory violations and no agent retries1.0 if clean; decays 0.20 per retry; 0.0 on any violation
statutory_compliance0.25No statutory_violations1.0 if list empty, else 0.0 (and triggers the hard cap)
grounding_score0.25% of substantive decision-trace nodes that carry an evidence_idNumerator: nodes with evidence_id; meta-orchestration nodes excluded
input_completeness0.25Required fields present in sanitized_profileFraction of required fields populated; FL null-credit docked 0.10

Deferred to v1.1 (4 signals — see §17): agent_agreement_signal, retry_inverse, numeric_consistency_score, council_agreement.

The formula

confidence_score = Σ (signal_i × 0.25)

  bounded to [0.05, 0.95]
  then hard-capped:
    if statutory_compliance < 1.0 → cap at 0.5

The hard cap is not a "soft penalty"; it is a deterministic ceiling. A statutorily non-compliant quote cannot produce a confidence_score above 0.5 no matter how high every other signal scores.

The augmentative LLM rationale (DEC-0012) → §17

An earlier design (DEC-0012) added an LLM call after the math to write a 2-3 sentence rationale_summary. v1.0 ships without this — the rationale is implicit in the per-dimension sub-scores. The full DEC lives in docs/decisions/deferred/0012-llm-confidence-explainer-augments-0004.md.

Why deterministic-first

  • A defensible signal, not a calibrated probability. The 0.0-1.0 range is meaningful within the system, but is not a literal probability. A reviewer should not interpret 0.82 as "82% likely to be correct" — it means "82% of the way along the in-system confidence dimension".
  • Weights are equal (0.25 each). The v1.0 4-signal version treats the four kept signals as co-equal load-bearing checks. Production would calibrate weights against an eval set of regulator-acceptable vs rejected quotes.
  • Hard cap preserves the gate. No matter what signals say, a statutorily non-compliant quote tops out at 0.5. The cap is what makes the score honest.

  • Pricing the wrong policy form → customer rejects the quote.
  • Recommending coverages the policy form doesn't carry → regulatory complaint, bad-faith claim.

▸ 15 · Assumptions

The Domain Choices Baked Into The System

Every multi-agent system makes domain choices that the architecture then assumes are true. Surfacing those choices is part of the audit chain — a reviewer should know which decisions were design assumptions and which were derived from data. This section enumerates the four big categories.

1 · Pricing-model basis

  • Multiplicative chain, not additive. Premium = base × scaling × wildfire × seismic × hurricane × flood × claims × pool × credit. Multipliers stack multiplicatively, not additively. Real rate-filings often mix the two; the system collapses to pure multiplicative for simplicity and traceability.
  • Base premium = state-statewide-median for a $250K reference dwelling. The state is the granularity unit. There is no per-carrier base premium and no sub-state geographic adjustment baked into base_premium; locality is captured via hazard multipliers.
  • Home-value scaling is linear at 0.18 per $100K above the $250K reference. Real rate-filings often use piecewise curves; the linear approximation is calibrated against published industry data but is not perfectly faithful to any single carrier's curve.
  • The ±25% imprecision band on the point estimate is the quote's communicated uncertainty. The customer sees premium_range = (low, high), where low = point × 0.75 and high = point × 1.25. This is a contract-driven choice; real underwriting refines further before binding.
  • Multipliers are independent. No correlation modeling between wildfire and seismic (or between hurricane and flood). A real CAT model would correlate. The system's simplification is acceptable because every multiplier has a defensible single-peril rationale.

2 · Coverage taxonomy

  • HO-3 (standard homeowners) is the default policy form. The system does not model HO-4 (renters), HO-6 (condo), HO-5 (open-peril both dwelling and contents), or DP-1/3 (dwelling fire) as primary outputs. FAIR Plan basic (DP-1) is referenced as a market route but not as a coverage taxonomy.
  • The standard coverage ratios are: Cov A = max(rebuild cost, lender floor, home_value); Cov B (other structures) = 10% of A; Cov C (personal property) = 50% of A; Cov D (loss of use) = 24 months in CA per §2051.5, 12 months elsewhere; Cov E (personal liability) = $300K recommended (uplifted from the $100K HO-3 default given typical pool/dog liability exposures); Cov F (medical payments) is collapsed to a default and not enumerated.
  • State-specific endorsements: CA → CEA earthquake companion; FL → CGCC mandatory + 4 hurricane deductible options + wind-mitigation advisory; SFHA-zone-mortgaged → NFIP flood. No other state-specific endorsements are modeled.
  • ISO HO-3 form (HO 00 03 05 11) is the reference contract for coverage definitions. The coverage taxonomy CSV maps free-text coverage names to ISO codes.

3 · Jurisdictional scope

  • Only CA and FL are supported. All other states route to the STATE-SUPPORTED short-circuit which emits an informational-only output. Adding a state requires (a) a new RAG corpus for that state's DOI/DFS content, (b) routing rules in the StatutoryAgent prompt, (c) new evidence_id patterns, (d) updates to the base_premium CSV, (e) updates to the cohort and hazard tools.
  • The two demo profiles cover the marquee compliance edge cases: CA Prop 103 credit-drop and FL §626.9741(7) null-credit neutral. A real production rollout would need more profiles per state to exercise other compliance branches.
  • Real per-customer geocoding is out of scope. When lat/lon is not provided, the system falls back to state-default coordinates (LA County for CA, Miami-Dade for FL). The hazard tools will still return real data for those defaults — just less precise than a true geocoded address. A production rollout would integrate Google/Mapbox geocoding.
  • The CA / FL split is hard-coded in scoring tables (EligibilityTriage), short-circuits (StatutoryAgent), and FL-only tools (citizens_benchmark, fl_hurricane_deductible, wind_mitigation_discount).

4 · Data provenance

  • Four of the five pricing tools read synthetic-but-calibrated tables. Only base_premium has real anchors for 2022 + 2025 (with synthesized 2026 projections). pricing_multiplier_lookup, home_value_scaling_factor, and citizens_benchmark are calibrated. cohort_benchmark is purely synthetic.
  • Two of the seven hazard tools may fall back to synthetic when FEMA's TLS handshake fails (FEMA NRI + FEMA NFHL). The synthetic snapshots are curated from real publicly-disclosed values; a synthetic_fallback: true flag is written into the cache file so any reviewer can grep.
  • All "real" data is curated subsets of public sources. No live data feeds run at quote time. The data was fetched once during initial seeding; subsequent refreshes require running data/scripts/fetch_real_data.py manually.
  • The architecture supports the swap. Production would replace synthetic tables with connectors to real rate-filing services (S&P Global, AM Best) and to per-insurer loss-cost data. Every tool's Pydantic IO signature is stable; the agents above the tools remain unaware of where the numbers come from.
▸ 16 · Trade-offs & Improvements

What I'd Do Differently With More Time

Every system at this stage is a snapshot of decisions made under a time and scope budget. This section names the trade-offs explicitly: what we built, what we cut, what we'd build next, and which limitations a regulator deserves to know about up front.

What I'd build next, ranked by leverage

  1. Replace synthetic pricing tables with real rate-filing connectors. Currently four of five pricing tools read calibrated approximations. Production should hook into S&P Global Market Intelligence (for industry rate-filing data) and per-insurer loss-cost APIs (for cohort percentiles). Tool signatures stay stable; only the underlying data sources change. Highest leverage because it converts the system from "defensible demo" to "actuarially binding".
  2. Hybrid retrieval beyond dense-only. The retriever's docstring claims "BM25 + dense + RRF + reranker"; the implementation is currently dense-only. Adding the BM25 leg + Reciprocal-Rank-Fusion + a cross-encoder reranker would improve precision on near-paraphrase statute queries. Currently masked by jurisdiction-tagged corpora being small enough that dense is good-enough.
  3. Broaden the Follow-up agent's keyword corpus router. The keyword matchers at followup_agent.py:19-33 miss questions that don't use the exact keywords. Captured runs show some follow-ups never trigger any corpus retrieval. A richer router (semantic similarity, or an LLM-picked corpus) would improve the answer quality on edge-case questions.
  4. Per-carrier pricing instead of state-level. Currently base_premium is one number per (state, year). Production should be (state, year, carrier). The architecture supports this; the data layer would need to expand.
  5. Real catastrophe modeling for hurricane / wildfire. The current wildfire and hurricane multipliers are scalar tiers (Low / Moderate / High / Very High). A real CAT model would produce probabilistic loss distributions and tail-VaR-aware pricing. Out of scope for this iteration — real CAT models are multi-million-dollar vendor products and the FEMA NRI + Citizens tier proxies are good-enough for the demo.
  6. LLM-as-Judge confidence calibration. The 4-signal confidence weights are equal in v1.0 (DEC-0004). Production should calibrate against an eval set of regulator-acceptable vs rejected quotes and re-introduce the 4 deferred signals — see §17. Same architecture; just need labelled data.
  7. Multi-axis counterfactual chains. Currently the Counterfactual agent forks once per question. Chaining forks ("what if I install hurricane shutters AND upgrade my roof AND raise my deductible to 5%?") would unlock richer "what should I do to lower my premium?" Q&A.
  8. Provider-snapshot pinning. The current setup uses OpenAI models without snapshot pinning. Production should pin to specific model snapshots so the same input produces the same output across rollouts — a requirement for audit-defensibility.

Known limitations to disclose to a regulator

  • Synthetic multipliers. Most pricing multipliers are calibrated approximations of public-source ranges, not pulled from real rate filings. The architecture is built for the swap; production would replace.
  • Dense-only retrieval. Hybrid BM25 + dense + RRF + reranker is specified but not yet implemented. Acceptable for small jurisdiction-tagged corpora; would not scale.
  • Hand-chosen confidence weights. No eval-set calibration. The 0.0-1.0 score is a defensible signal, not a calibrated probability.
  • Two-state scope. CA and FL only. All other states get an informational-only output.
  • State-default geocoding. Real per-property lat/lon is not collected; hazard lookups use state-default coordinates.
  • Pinned-snapshot drift. Without explicit OpenAI snapshot pinning, the same input may produce different LLM outputs across provider rollouts. LangSmith captures the drift so it is visible, but it is not eliminated.

Why Provenance Honesty Is The System's Strength

A reviewer who reads this entire document and grasps only one idea should grasp this: the system's strength is not that every number is real. It isn't. Of the five pricing tools, four are calibrated approximations of real industry data and the fifth is purely synthesized to give the Validator a peer-comparison band to challenge against. None of the tools fetch a live data feed at quote time.

The system's strength is that this honesty is visible from the outside in. Every dollar of premium carries an evidence_id. Every evidence_id appears in a documented CSV/JSON row. Every CSV/JSON file ships with either a per-row source column or a top-level description field. The provenance doc lists which tables are real anchors, which are calibrated, and which are pure synthesis. A regulator auditing a quote can grep an evidence_id and trace it to a row and from there to a public source — or to a documented note that says "calibrated synthetic." Either way, the audit chain holds.

Three takeaways

  1. Every dollar is traceable to a row in a documented table. The factor chain shown to the customer carries the same evidence_id values the auditor reads in the CSV. No "magic numbers" in code, no LLM-generated multipliers, no derivable values that aren't grounded.
  2. Compliance contracts live in data, not in scattered if-statements. Prop 103's drop-credit requirement and §626.9741(7)'s neutral-credit requirement are enforced by two specific entries in pricing_multipliers.json with value 1.00 and their statutes' citations as evidence_ids. The Compliance Council holds VETO power as a second backstop; the regression test (tests/verify_replacements.py) is a third backstop in CI.
  3. The architecture is built for the swap. The provenance contract explicitly anticipates that production would replace the curated multipliers JSON with a connector to a real rate-filing service, and would replace the cohort table with real insurer loss-cost data. Tool signatures stay stable; the underlying data layer swaps cleanly.
