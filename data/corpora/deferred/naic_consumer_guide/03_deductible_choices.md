# Choosing a deductible

The deductible is the amount the insured pays out-of-pocket on each covered claim before the insurer's obligation begins. Higher deductibles lower premium but raise at-risk exposure. NAIC consumer guidance recommends selecting a deductible the household could comfortably absorb in a typical year.

**All-other-perils deductible.** A flat dollar amount ($500, $1,000, $2,500, $5,000 are typical) that applies to most covered losses other than catastrophe perils. Each $500 step up the ladder typically reduces premium 5-10%.

**Hurricane deductible (FL, NC, SC, TX, LA, MS, AL).** A separate, usually percentage-based deductible that applies only to losses caused by named tropical-system events. In Florida, Fla. Stat. §627.701 mandates the insurer offer at least four options: $500 flat (when dwelling <= $250K), 2% of Coverage A, 5% of Coverage A, and 10% of Coverage A. The Coverage Agent should surface all four.

**Wind/hail deductible (Midwest).** Some carriers in tornado-prone states impose a separate wind/hail deductible (typically 1-2% of Coverage A) to manage convective-storm losses.

**Earthquake deductible.** California Earthquake Authority (CEA) policies use percentage deductibles of 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, or 25% of dwelling. CEA premium scales steeply downward with higher deductibles (a 25% deductible carries roughly 80% of the premium of a 15% deductible). Recommendation should consider construction era and foundation type.

**Flood deductible.** NFIP standard policy uses fixed dollar deductibles ($1,000 - $10,000) per the Standard Flood Insurance Policy. Private flood deductibles vary widely.

The economic crossover for a deductible step up (e.g., $1K -> $2.5K) is typically reached after about 2-3 claim-free years; insureds with >2 claims in 3 years are typically better off with the lower deductible. The Counterfactual agent's "what if I raised my deductible to $X" path runs this analysis on the customer's actual numbers.
